How AI
actually works.

Every answer from an AI travels through eight invisible layers — from electrons on a power grid, to silicon the width of a virus, to a trillion numbers in a model, to the words on your screen. Scroll, and we'll peel them apart one by one.

01

It starts with electricity.

Before any "intelligence" exists, there is just power. Coal, gas, nuclear, solar, hydro — all converted into a steady river of electrons flowing into a building the size of a shopping mall.

~3 Wh
a single ChatGPT-style query — roughly 10× a Google search.
~50 GWh
estimated electricity to train GPT-4 — enough to power ~5,000 US homes for a year.
1–2%
of global electricity is consumed by data centers today. Some forecasts put AI alone at 8–12% of US power by 2030.
700 W
one NVIDIA H100 GPU running flat-out. A modern AI cluster has tens of thousands of them.

Why does it matter? Because everything above this layer — chips, models, your chatbot — is a way of converting raw electricity into useful computation. The cheaper and cleaner the watts, the cheaper and faster the AI.

02

The electricity moves transistors.

That power is fed into specialized chips called GPUs (graphics processing units) and TPUs. They were originally designed to render video games, but they turned out to be shockingly good at the math behind neural networks: multiplying enormous grids of numbers in parallel.

80 B
transistors on a single NVIDIA H100. Each is just a few atoms wide (4 nm process).
989 TFLOPS
peak throughput — that's ~989 trillion math operations every second. Per chip.
~$30k
price tag of one H100. The newer B200 is roughly 2–3× faster.
~90%
of the world's advanced AI chips are fabricated by a single company, TSMC, in Taiwan.

A modern AI chip is one of the most complex objects humans have ever manufactured. It is assembled atom-by-atom using ultraviolet light bounced off mirrors polished to within a few atoms of perfectly flat. There are only a handful of factories on Earth that can do it.

03

Thousands of chips, wired into one brain.

A single chip isn't enough. To train and serve a frontier model, you bolt tens of thousands of them into racks, then connect those racks with absurdly fast networking so they can act as one giant machine. This is what an "AI supercomputer" actually is: a building.

8 GPUs
in one server (an "HGX" node). Eight of these chips alone cost ~$300,000.
900 GB/s
NVLink bandwidth between GPUs inside a server — faster than your SSD by 1000×.
100,000+
GPUs in the largest single training clusters (e.g. xAI's Colossus, Microsoft × OpenAI).
100 MW
power draw of a large AI campus — about the same as a small city. Cooled with water or immersion fluid.

When a model is training, every GPU in the building is constantly shipping its progress to every other GPU. The interconnect — the wires between chips — is often the real bottleneck, not the chips themselves. That's why these buildings look more like networking nightmares than the server rooms of the cloud era.

04

You feed it the internet.

Hardware is the body. Data is the food. To train an LLM you collect a colossal text corpus — a scrape of the public internet, books, code, scientific papers, forums — and chop it into small pieces called tokens (roughly ¾ of a word each).

~13 T
tokens estimated for GPT-4's training set. Roughly 10 billion books worth of text.
0.75 word
average length of a single token. "Unbelievable" might be 3 tokens.
Common Crawl
a public web scrape (~250 billion pages) — the backbone of most LLM corpora.
quality > quantity
curating, deduplicating, and filtering the data matters more than sheer size.

The model never sees words. It sees integer IDs — one number per token. Every sentence you've ever typed, every book ever written, gets compressed into a long stream of those integers before the math even begins.

05

The model is just a grid of numbers.

An LLM is, physically, a giant pile of floating-point numbers — its parameters or weights. There is no "knowledge base", no database of facts inside. There is only this grid. The architecture that connects those numbers is called a transformer.

~1.7 T
parameters estimated in GPT-4 (mixture-of-experts). Each parameter is a single number.
attention
the core trick: every token looks at every other token, deciding what to pay attention to.
embeddings
each token becomes a vector — a point in a high-dimensional space where meaning lives.
layers
dozens or hundreds of stacked blocks, each refining the meaning a little more.

Picture the model as a sieve with a hundred floors. A sentence enters the top as raw tokens. On each floor it gets remixed: tokens "talk" to each other, related ideas pull toward each other in space, irrelevant ones fade. By the bottom floor, the model has a confident guess about what comes next.

06

It learns by being wrong, trillions of times.

Training is brutally simple. Show the model a chunk of text with the last word hidden, let it guess, then nudge every one of its parameters slightly in the direction that would've made the guess better. Repeat. About a quadrillion times.

pretraining
"predict the next token." This is where the model absorbs the world.
fine-tuning
teach it to follow instructions, answer politely, refuse harmful requests.
RLHF
humans rank the model's answers. The model learns what humans actually prefer.
3–6 months
wall-clock training time for a frontier model on a 25,000+ GPU cluster.

The astonishing part: no one programmed grammar, facts, or reasoning into the model. They emerged as side effects of getting really good at one tiny task — guess the next token — at a scale no human could ever practice.

07

When you talk to it, it's just guessing the next word.

The frozen, finished model sits on a fleet of GPUs. You type a question. It gets tokenized, run through every layer of the network, and the model spits out a probability distribution over every possible next token. It picks one. Then it does the whole thing again. And again. That's "inference."

one token
at a time. The model has no plan — it just keeps choosing the next word.
temperature
a dial that controls randomness. Low = boring & deterministic, high = creative & weird.
context window
how many tokens of memory it carries — 200k tokens ≈ a 500-page novel.
streaming
the typewriter effect you see isn't a UI gimmick — that's the model producing tokens live.

So when an AI seems to "think before answering," what's really happening is more tokens flowing — it's literally thinking out loud, one syllable at a time, and using its own output as fresh input for the next guess.

08

And finally — it reaches you.

On top of the model sits a thin layer of software: a chat UI, a coding assistant, an agent that can browse the web or run code. This is the only part most people ever see — but it's standing on every layer below it.

chat
ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini — the most familiar surface. ~1 billion+ users combined.
coding
Cursor, Copilot, Claude Code — the model edits files and runs commands for you.
agents
models that call tools, browse the web, book travel, write entire codebases.
multimodal
the same trick — predict the next token — but tokens now include images, audio, video.

Next time you type a message and a stream of words appears: remember that every one of those words came from electrons → silicon → a datacenter → trillions of tokens → a trillion-parameter model → an inference step → a network response → your screen.

That's the stack. That's how AI actually works.
